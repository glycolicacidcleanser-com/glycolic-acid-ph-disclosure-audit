Glycolic acid pH disclosure audit

Compiled by Rodger, editor, glycolicacidcleanser.com · read on 5 September 2026 · CC BY 4.0

The percentage on a glycolic acid label says how much glycolic acid is in the formula. How much of it is in the free, un-ionised form depends on the formula's pH, so a percentage without a pH is half the information. This page records, brand by brand, whether the maker publishes that pH on its own product page.

  • 8 of 26readable brand pages state a numeric pH
  • 5make a pH claim such as "pH-balanced" and give no number
  • 13say nothing about pH on the page; 1 of them later gave an approximate figure when asked by email
  • 31brands tracked; 5 could not be read on a brand site and are left out of the 26

The full analysis, including what a published pH range does and does not tell a shopper, is on the glycolic acid statistics page. To turn a label percentage and a pH into an estimated free acid figure, use the free acid calculator.

Method

  1. For each tracked product, the brand's own website was read on 5 September 2026.
  2. A pH counts as published only when the brand's own page states it as a number. Marketing language alone is recorded as a claim with no number.
  3. Where a value was re-checked on a later date, the note says so.

Free acid at a stated pH follows the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation at glycolic acid's pKa of 3.83: free acid (%) = label % ÷ (1 + 10^(pH − 3.83)). This is arithmetic from a stated pH, not a lab measurement. The method was published by cosmetic chemist Michelle Wong at Lab Muffin Beauty Science; what this audit adds is each brand's own published pH.

Caveats

  • A snapshot from 5 September 2026. Brands change their pages, and it has not been recomputed since. The one later addition is YEOUTH, whose customer service answered our email on 15 November 2026 with an approximate pH of 2.
  • One product per brand, not each brand's whole range.
  • Brands selling glycolic acid products on Amazon US, not the whole market.
